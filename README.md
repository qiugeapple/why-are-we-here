# why-are-we-here
Don Ihde’s theory of technological media argues that technology is never a neutral tool. Instead, it shapes human perception and can constrain or expand human imagination and creative boundaries. Throughout my undergraduate studies, I have witnessed AI evolve from an auxiliary tool into an underlying medium guiding design workflows. I have also deeply experienced how a lack of media awareness limits designers’ creative capacity.

Over just three years, design pedagogy has undergone transformative change. In my first year, essays, research and visual projects relied entirely on manual labour: handwritten drafts, manual literature collection and original sketching. By the second year, AI had become a mainstream tool for information retrieval and organisation, drastically cutting repetitive research work. In the third year, AI penetrated the front end of creative practice, supporting brainstorming, sketch generation and visual exploration. The rapid advancement of AI has shifted designers’ core responsibility from hands‑on production to judgement, selection and iterative refinement.

I have recognised AI’s great strengths. It can rapidly translate vague, fragmented intuitive ideas into polished visual outputs, shortening the path from concept to initial draft. Yet fundamental challenges emerge. More importantly, media shapes designers’ imagination, a lesson I learned directly from visual communication training. Reliance on print and planar tools confined my creative thinking to a two‑dimensional framework. My concepts, composition and storytelling were bounded by static visuals. Like many peers, I lacked understanding of code, interaction and dynamic media. My designs stopped at visual prototypes. As a visual communication and UI/UX student, I could craft interface aesthetics, layouts and motion posters, yet I could not build working, functional systems. I could only imagine how something *looks*, not how it *operates*.

During group collaboration on my study project, I also noticed a clear imbalance between artists and programmers. Programmers could confidently claim they had *completed* the project, while visual designers like me could only say we had *participated* in it. This gap arises because programmers understand the underlying medium and can bring ideas to life, whereas designers who only create static visuals remain confined to the surface layer of the product.

This is the cost of media illiteracy: you can only create forms you understand. Design is not mere visual beautification. It is creative problem‑solving that conveys emotion and value. Without technical languages to translate ideas, creative expression becomes constrained, limiting implementation and the definition of core design value. This is vital for retaining agency amid AI. AI and code are ultimately design aids; they carry no inherent meaning. Humans give tools purpose and direction.

During my graduation project, I further observed my peers’ real‑world struggles. Even when AI quickly generated complete design assets, the creative process remained difficult. Take a public‑science design project on the Eight‑Section Brocade for older adults as an example. My classmate could prompt AI to produce numerous sketches, layouts and promotional materials. However, critical problems persisted: verifying whether AI outputs match the core knowledge of this elderly fitness guide, checking that they communicate accessible health information, selecting visuals suitable for older users’ cognition and visual habits, maintaining the project’s public welfare mission, and correcting AI inaccuracies, biased information and generic aesthetics. AI can endlessly produce visual materials, yet it cannot make design decisions. This taught me that in the age of AI, designers’ core value no longer lies in execution skills such as drawing, layout and modelling. Instead, it rests on higher‑order abilities: defining content, making aesthetic choices, maintaining logical coherence and setting project direction.

Therefore, systematic learning of programming alongside AI is essential. It fills technical gaps, breaks the mental limits imposed by print‑centred design, empowers me to retain creative control, builds a translation channel for my design thinking, and enables deeper, implementable and innovative design work. Learning programming is not about competing with AI at code production; it is about understanding media so I can guide technology rather than be limited by it.
